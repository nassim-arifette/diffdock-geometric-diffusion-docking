Docking by denoising

DiffDock-L places a drug-like molecule in its protein pocket by running a diffusion backwards, from noise to a pose. It proposes ten such poses. This project asks what to do with them.

In short

DiffDock treats molecular docking as generative modelling: it samples where and how a ligand sits in a protein by denoising its position, orientation and flexible bonds. A second network then ranks the samples by confidence.

Generating poses and choosing one are different problems. On the run shown here, none of the ten candidates lands within 2 Å of the crystal pose, and every one of them collides with the protein. No ranking can fix that pool.

The project builds an auditable pipeline to measure both failures separately, and a frozen pilot that compares four ways of choosing among the same candidates. Its results are not in yet; this page shows the method on real outputs and states the protocol in advance.

The pose has three kinds of freedom

The protein is held rigid. What remains to decide about the ligand is where its centre sits, a translation in ℝ³; how it is turned, a rotation in SO(3); and how each rotatable bond is twisted, one angle per torsion. DiffDock adds noise to all three and learns to remove it [1].

The ligand below is the crystal pose of 1A0Q’s ligand, a phosphonate with 23 heavy atoms and 11 torsions in DiffDock’s sense, bonds whose rotation moves more than one atom. Push it around and watch two numbers: how far it is from where it belongs, and whether it starts to overlap the protein.

Running the diffusion backwards

Sampling starts from a random pose: the ligand dropped somewhere near the protein, randomly turned and twisted. A score network then proposes, at each of about twenty steps, a small update of translation, rotation and torsions, with the noise level shrinking each time. Positions are only ever changed through those three kinds of moves, so bond lengths and angles stay those of the input conformer.

These are the real recorded frames of the ten candidates from one DiffDock-L run on a T4 GPU. Pick a candidate and scrub through its steps.

Ten candidates, none of them right

A confidence model ranks the samples. It is trained to predict closeness to the experimental pose, not binding affinity, and it does not check physics. PoseBusters [3] does: bond lengths, angles, flat rings, internal clashes, and clashes with the protein.

Here the confidence ranking puts a 5.2 Å pose first; the closest candidate in the pool, at 3.7 Å, is ranked eighth. Every pose passes the internal chemistry checks and fails the two protein-contact checks. The crystal ligand itself passes them against the same prepared receptor, so the validator is not the problem.

So the run fails twice over. The generator produced no near-native pose, and the poses it did produce are physically impossible. A filter that keeps only valid poses would keep none; a better ranker would still pick a wrong pose. Separating these two failures is the whole point of the pilot.

The pilot: four ways to choose

The protocol was written before any pilot result. It freezes complexes from the 308-case PoseBusters benchmark, generates 20 DiffDock-L candidates per complex and seed, and then compares four selectors on exactly the same candidates. No selector ever sees the reference pose.

The primary outcome is a top-1 pose that is both within 2 Å and physically valid. RMSD-only and validity-only success, top-5, the best pose in each pool (“oracle” quality), abstentions and errors are reported alongside, so a selector cannot look good by refusing hard cases.

  • 5 development and 20 evaluation complexes, 3 seeds each. Seeds are repeated measurements of the same complex, not new data; uncertainty is bootstrapped by complex.
  • The splits are PDB-disjoint, not protein-family-disjoint, and the checkpoint’s training overlap with the benchmark is not audited.
  • Receptors with metals or covalent links are excluded, and waters, ions and other heteroatoms are removed, so nothing here speaks to metal, covalent, cofactor or water-mediated binding.
  • Filtering, rescoring and refinement are established methods. DiffDock-Glide and Matcha already combine them with diffusion docking. The contribution is a transparent comparison and failure analysis at a fixed generation budget.

What the original course project got wrong

The original course report is kept as a course artifact, with a revision note. It docked a ligand into 6AGT that is not that structure’s crystal ligand, read the confidence score as if it measured affinity, and treated a physically plausible pose as a synthesizable one. Its plots are not a traceable baseline. The revised pipeline records the upstream commit, weights, inputs, seeds, effective parameters, GPU and timings of every run, with hashes.
